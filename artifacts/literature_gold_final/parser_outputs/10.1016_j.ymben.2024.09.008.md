## Architecting a Transcriptional Repressor-based Genetic Inverter for Tryptophan Derived Pathway Regulation in Escherichia coli

Xinyu Gong1, Yuxi Teng1, Jianli Zhang1, Qi Gan1, Ming Song2, Ameen Alaraj1, Peter Kner2, Yajun Yan1,*

- 1.School of Chemical, Materials and Biomedical Engineering, College of Engineering, The University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA.
- 2.School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, College of Engineering, The University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA.


#### Abstract

Efficient microbial cell factories require intricate and precise metabolic regulations for optimized production, which can be significantly aided by implementing regulatory genetic circuits with versatile functions. However, constructing functionally diverse genetic circuits in host strains is challenging. Especially, functional diversification based on transcriptional repressors has been rarely explored due to the difficulty in inverting their repression properties. To address this, we proposed a design logic to create transcriptional repressor-based genetic inverters for functional enrichment. As proof of concept, a tryptophan-inducible genetic inverter was constructed by integrating two sets of transcriptional repressors, PtrpO1-TrpR1 and PtetO1-TetR. In this genetic inverter, the repression of TetR towards PtetO1could be alleviated by the tryptophan-TrpR1 complex in the presence of tryptophan, leading to the activated output. Subsequently, we optimized the dynamic performance of the inverter and constructed tryptophan-triggered dynamic activation systems. Further coupling of the original repression function of PtrpO1-TrpR1 with inverter variants realized the tryptophan-triggered bifunctional regulation system. Finally, the dynamic regulation systems enabled tryptophan production monitoring. These systems also remarkably increased the titers of the tryptophan derivatives tryptamine and violacein by 2.0-fold and 7.4-fold, respectively. The successful design and application of the genetic inverter enhanced the applicability of transcriptional repressors.

#### Introduction

Microbial cell factories are engineered to harbor biosynthetic pathways for the efficient production of desired compounds (Cho et al., 2022; Gustavsson and Lee, 2016). However, overexpression of crucial synthetic genes and the deletion of competing pathways often lead to impaired cell growth and imbalanced carbon flow, resulting in low production yield (Fang et al., 2020; Prelich, 2012). Implementing regulatory genetic circuits is an effective alternative to precisely modulating cell metabolism and relieving the detrimental effects of pathway modifications, enhancing the productivity of microbial cell factories (Jiang et al., 2022). One strategy is establishing regulatory circuits based on transcriptional factors (TFs). Microorganisms possess TFs, a type of regulatory protein, to balance the varying intracellular metabolisms and adapt to the changing extracellular environments during cell growth (Schallmey et al., 2014). TFs have been well-explored due to their unique signalresponsive properties, diverse regulatory mechanisms, and wide distributions among species. Both intracellular and extracellular signals, such as metabolites, temperature, and pH, can act as signals to trigger TFs and initiate the regulation at the transcriptional level (Kim et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2024; Pham et al., 2022). Inspired by the inducible capabilities, various signal-responsive TFs-based biosensors have been generated as genetic tools for the detection of metal ions or pollutants (Özyurt et al., 2019), the dynamic regulation of microbial metabolic networks (Zou et al., 2024), and the high-throughput screening of functional variants (Kaczmarek and Prather, 2021), among other applications.

The diverse mechanisms of TFs represent a unique opportunity for biosensor development (Teng et al., 2022). TFs can be categorized into three subclasses according to their final output: transcriptional activators, transcriptional repressors, and bifunctional TFs (Deng et al., 2022). Bifunctional TFs hold great potential for intricate metabolic regulations due to their diverse functions. For example, the cAMP receptor in E.coli, CRP (Kolb et al., 1993), is a complex regulatory protein involved in global regulation networks with activation and repression functions. The CRP-cAMP complex recognizes DNA binding boxes with a 5’ TGTGA 3’ motif, leading to up- or down-regulation of target genes by interfering with RNA polymerase binding in different phases (Kolb et al., 1993). In comparison, transcriptional activators and transcriptional repressors are mono-functional regulators, and expanded functions are required to achieve precise metabolic regulation and efficient microbial production. Transcriptional activators are well-studied TFs and exhibit an activated or enhanced output after responding to the signal, which has been proven to be easily manipulated due to their straightforward activation mechanisms. For instance, the pBAD-AraC is a well-known activation system (Soisson et al., 1997), in which the presence of the L-arabinose-AraC complex disrupts the DNA looping and enables the activated transcription of the downstream gene under the control of the PBADpromoter (Soisson et al., 1997). Moreover, transcriptional activators not only work independently

but also can be coupled with CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) as a functionally inverted system with expanded repression function for metabolic engineering applications. To construct a transcriptional activator-based genetic inverter, sgRNA can be directly controlled by the transcriptional activator to achieve repression property. Wang et al. engineered a 3-hydroxybutyrate (3HB) responsive activator HpdR and established a tunable CRISPRi system by expressing variable-length sgRNAs after PhpdHpromoter in a CRISPR dCas9integrated strain. Thus they effectively screened the accArepression target for improved 1,3-BDO yield and ultimately achieved a production of 22.66 g/L in a 1-L fermenter (Wang et al., 2021).

Conversely, transcriptional repressors present a repressed or attenuated output after receiving the signal. For instance, the well-characterized transcriptional repressor, TrpR (Arvidson et al., 1986), shows aporepressor properties upon interacting with tryptophan. When tryptophan combines with the TrpR dimers, the transcription under the control of the PtrpOpromoter is gradually inhibited as tryptophan levels increase (Arvidson et al., 1986). However, applications of transcriptional repressors are limited by their repression mechanisms. First, gene overexpression strategies are often given priority over gene repression in regulating microbial production (Hirasawa and Shimizu, 2016). Besides, leaky expression of the repressors is quite common, which would cause compromised repression efficacy in regulating cellular metabolism and lead to false positives during high-throughput screening. Therefore, it is crucial to engineer transcriptional repressors for improved applicability and expanded functions, especially given the finite TFs-based biosensors for specific metabolites.

Functionally flipped transcriptional repressor-based genetic inverters are underexplored, as assembling genetic elements for expanded circuit functions and amplified output signals is challenging. In this study, we established a tryptophan-inducible transcriptional repressorbased genetic inverter in Escherichia coli(E. coli) and successfully attained the activation function. The inverter employed a tryptophan-inducible PtrpO1-TrpR1 repression system and a PtetO1-TetR repression system (Ellefson et al., 2018; Lutz and Bujard, 1997). We began by detailing the design logic and constructing the initial Inverter_V1 that showed insignificant expression and inconspicuous activation. To ensure a significant activation output, we assessed the effector response range of each repressor system to enhance compatibility between them. The engineered Inverter_V1.1 and Inverter_V1.2 showed improved expression levels with 3.2-fold and 14.7-fold activation, respectively. Subsequently, we optimized the inverter by manipulating key genetic elements, resulting in variants with improved dynamic performance and activation strengths. The selected inverter variants were further integrated into a tryptophan-producing strain to establish tryptophan-triggered dynamic activation systems. We also coupled the inherent repression function of PtrpO1-TrpR1 with the inverter to create tryptophan-triggered bifunctional regulation systems and characterized them by simultaneously measuring repressed GFP and activated RFP output at both the cell population and single-cell levels. Finally, the tryptophan-triggered regulation systems were successfully applied to monitor tryptophan production and dynamic regulation of tryptophan derivative production. The dynamic upregulation of the tryptamine pathway and bifunctional regulation of the violacein pathway enhanced production titers by 2.0-fold and 7.4-fold, respectively. Our results demonstrate

the functionality and efficiency of the tryptophan-inducible genetic inverter and its derived regulation systems in coordinating heterologous pathway expression and highlight the advantages of employing regulatory genetic circuits in microbial production.

#### Results

###### 1. Design of the Tryptophan-inducible TrpR-TetR Genetic Inverter

To engineer complex cell metabolism, versatile regulatory functions are commonly desired. The single repression functionality of transcriptional repressors often impedes their application in many scenarios. Herein, we proposed a universal design logic for transcriptional repressor-based genetic inverters to broaden their functionality (Figure 1a and 1b). Our design incorporated two transcriptional repressors for the inverter construction. Specifically, transcriptional repressor 1 is ligand-inducible and responsible for detecting inducers and controlling the expression of transcriptional repressor 2. Transcriptional repressor 2 executes the repression function directly by regulator-operator interaction and performs the inverted signal output. In the absence of the inducer, transcriptional repressor 2 is constantly expressed and maintains tight repression towards the output signal (Figure 1a). Upon addition of the inducer, the complex of transcriptional repressor 1 and inducer would repress the expression of transcriptional repressor 2. This would alleviate the repression imposed by transcriptional repressor 2 to its corresponding promoter, consequently causing activation output (Figure 1b).

As proof of concept, we selected tryptophan-inducible PtrpO1-TrpR1 and PtetO1-TetR repressors to develop the proposed inverter. In the PtrpO1-TrpR1 repression system, the TrpR1-tryptophan complex binds to the PtrpO1promoter upon tryptophan addition and represses the expression of the downstream gene (Gong et al., 2022). In the PtetO1-TetR repression system, TetR binds to the PtetO1promoter and directly inhibits the downstream gene expression (Lutz and Bujard, 1997). In our designed inverter, TrpR1 is designated as the transcriptional repressor 1, which is responsible for detecting tryptophan and controlling the expression of TetR. TetR serves as the transcriptional repressor 2 to invert the final output. In the absence of tryptophan, TetR would express constantly and repress the PtetO1promoter due to the lack of repression of the TrpR1-tryptophan complex, and the genetic inverter shows no activation output (Figure 1c). In the presence of tryptophan, the TrpR1-tryptophan complex suppresses TetR expression and alleviates the repression of TetR towards the PtetO1promoter. This ultimately results in tryptophan-inducible activation output (Figure 1d).

###### 2. Construction and Characterization of the Tryptophan-inducible TrpR-TetR Genetic Inverter

To construct the inverter, we selected the PtrpO1-TrpR1 repressor variant PtrpO1(G3C)TrpR1(V58K) based on its low leaky expression and high repression efficacy when induced by tryptophan in our previous study (Gong et al., 2022). First, the regulator protein TrpR1(V58K) was constitutively expressed in the low-copy number plasmid under the control of the Plpp0.03constitutive promoter. Next, the corresponding promoter PtrpO1(G3C)was placed in the medium-copy number plasmid to control TetR expression

for the incorporation of the PtetO1-TetR repression system. Finally, PtetO1promotercontrolled RFP was constructed in the high-copy number plasmid as reporter plasmid for activation output. The assembly of the genetic elements resulted in the tryptophan-inducible Inverter_V1 (Figure 1c and 1d). Next, Inverter_V1 was characterized in strain XG1 (E. coliBW25113 F’ ΔtrpR ΔtnaA) for proof-of-concept. Unfortunately, no significant RFP activation was observed after adding a gradient concentration of tryptophan in fluorescence assays (Figure 1e). We deduced that the two transcriptional repressors inside Inverter_V1 did not coordinate well by such assembly.

To troubleshoot Inverter_V1, we investigated the two repression systems separately by fluorescence assays. Firstly, we tested the tryptophan-inducible repression property of the constitutively expressed PtrpO1(G3C)-Plpp0.03-TrpR1(V58K) system. The results showed that the constitutively expressed repression system could respond to tryptophan as the concertation changed from 0 to 125 mg/L (Figure S1a) and even exhibited better repression efficacy compared with our previously developed PtrpO1(G3C)-PLlacO1-TrpR1(V58K) system (Gong et al., 2022). We then evaluated the tunability of the PtetO1-TetR repression system by using a well-established IPTG-inducible PLlacO1promoter to control the expression of TetR and using the PtetO1promoter to control the reporter GFP (Figure S1b). Ideally, higher IPTG induction would increase TetR levels, leading to stronger repression of GFP in a dose-dependent manner. However, our results indicated extreme repression at low IPTG concentrations (Figure S1b). The expected dose-dependent response was not observed. We inferred that the stable and strong binding affinity between TetR and the PtetO1 promoter caused stringent repression, which led to the dysfunction of Inverter_V1. To solve this problem, we introduced two protein degradation tags DAS and LAA (McGinness et al., 2006), and attached them to TetR to form PtetO1-TetR repression system variants. With different protein degradation rates, we hypothesized that the variants would lead to reduced TetR protein stability and relieve the stringent repression. After characterizing PtetO1-TetR repression system variants in strain XG1, we observed the dose-dependent repression manner when TetR expression was induced by 0 to 50 μM IPTG among two groups (Figure S1c). Especially, the repression system with TetR_LAA showed higher GFP intensity upon induction of 10 μM IPTG, indicating a faster degradation rate of the LAA tag. This result suggested that the stringent repression of TetR was sufficiently relieved by attaching the LAA degradation tag. With degradation tags, the PtetO1-TetR system can gradually downregulate the gene expression controlled by the PtetO1promoter instead of stringent repression. The strategy could be potentially used to solve the Inverter_V1 dysfunction.

Next, we introduced TetR_DAS and TetR_LAA into Inverter_V1, yielding inverter variants Inverter_V1.1 and Inverter_V1.2, and evaluated their activation functions in fluorescence assays. As shown in Figure 1e, both variants exhibited improved RFP outputs compared to Inverter_V1 upon 0 to 500 mg/L tryptophan induction. Remarkably, Inverter_V1.2 demonstrated a 14.7-fold dynamic range with minimal leaky expression (45.8±4.7 a.u.) and a high expression level (672.6±142.2 a.u.). While Inverter_V1.1 exhibited a 3.2-fold dynamic range with a lower expression level ranging from 40.8±7.8 a.u. to 129.6±53.0 a.u. The results indicated that introducing degradation tags significantly improved inverter performance and solved the dysfunction. Furthermore, characterizing the inverter validated

###### 3. Exploring and Fine-tuning Key Genetic Elements of Genetic Inverter for Amplified Activation Outputs

Given the low expression level of our inverter variants, we identified two potential factors that contributed to the results. On the one hand, the leaky expression of PtrpO1(G3C)Plpp0.03-TrpR1(V58K) repression system led to excessive TetR accumulation. This subsequently caused continuous tight repression towards the PtetO1promoter, thereby resulting in the low activation output. On the other hand, the weak PtetO1promoter strength directly resulted in the low activation output. To address these issues, we optimized the inverter variants by reducing TetR abundance and improving the PtetO1promoter strength for enhanced activation output.

TetR abundance was regarded as one crucial tunable element. We endeavored to mitigate TetR accumulation by reducing the leaky expression of PtrpO1(G3C)-Plpp0.03TrpR1(V58K) repression system. Then, a series of low-strength ribosome binding sites (RBS) were generated by randomly mutating the loci 6, 7, 8, and 9 (refer to the iGEM library (http://parts.igem.org/Part:BBa_K1676100)) of the wildtype RBS individually for lower PtrpO1(G3C)promoter strength (Figure 2b). Next, we introduced these RBS variants into the PtrpO1(G3C)-Plpp0.03-TrpR1(V58K) repression system, resulting in a series of repression system variants for fluorescence assays. All variants maintained similar repression efficiency but showed lower leakiness than the wild type (Figure S2). As the RBS strength decreased, the repression system variants exhibited lower leaky expression. According to the reduced varying levels of leakiness, we selected RBS variants RBS(G7T), RBS(A8G), and RBS(G9C) for inverter optimization (Figure 2a). Inverter variants were designated based on their RBS modifications: those with RBS(G7T) as Inverter_V1.1.1 and Inverter_V1.2.1; those with RBS(A8G) as Inverter_V1.1.2 and Inverter_V1.2.2; and those with RBS(G9C) as Inverter_V1.1.3 and Inverter_V1.2.3. Upon induction with tryptophan, the inverter variants demonstrated a spectrum of activation strengths, ranging from low to high dynamic levels (Figure 2c). The inverter variant with the low-strength RBS showed a higher activation strength. Notably, Inverter_V1.2.1 generated by introducing the loweststrength RBS(G7T) to Inverter_V1.2, exhibited the most enhanced activation output of 2208±5.0 a.u., representing a 5-fold increase from the baseline of 440±21.1 a.u. (Figure 2c). This variant could potentially serve as high-expression-level activation circuits in pathway regulation. Additionally, a low-expression-level Inverter_V1.1.1 variant with a 7.38-fold dynamic range was selected to enrich the diversity of the inverter repository for broader applicability. The results demonstrated that reduced TetR abundance had a positive effect on amplifying activation outputs.

The PtetO1promoter strength was considered as the other crucial tunable element to further amplify the activation outputs. To validate this, we introduced random mutations in the −10 region of the PtetO1promoter and its RBS region to enhance the gene expression level. We evaluated the relative RFP expression levels of the mutant promoters

compared to the wildtype PtetO1promoter (Figure 2e). The PtetO1-RBS(A2C) promoter variant exhibited the highest RFP expression, with a 2.21-fold increase over the wildtype. Additionally, RFP expression controlled by PtetO1(N4–2)and PtetO1(N6–8)promoter variants exhibited a 1.7-fold and 1.8-fold increase, respectively. The findings showed great potential for enhancing the inverter activation outputs. To diversify the activation performances of the inverter, all obtained PtetO1promoter variants with distinct strengths were introduced into Inverter_V1.1.1 and Inverter_V1.2.1 (Figure 2d). Inverter _V1.1.1 with PtetO1variants were designated as Inverter _V1.1.1a-g, and Inverter _V1.2.1 with PtetO1variants were designated as Inverter _V1.2.1a-g. We characterized the inverter variants with gradient tryptophan in strain XG1. Notably, Inverter_V1.2.1e reached the highest RFP expression (2316.3±75.5 a.u.) with moderate leaky expression (483.8±22.6 a.u.) among Inverter_V1.2.1 variants and showed a 4.8-fold dynamic range (Figure 2g). Inverter_V1.1.1e reached the highest RFP expression (1485.1±48.5 a.u.) with low leaky expression (218.6±40.5 a.u.) among Inverter_V1.1.1 variants and showed a 6.8-fold dynamic range (Figure 2f).

In summary, we successfully amplified the inverter outputs by reducing the TetR abundance and engineering the PtetO1promoter and its RBS. The resulting inverter variants showed diverse activation strength, which holds great promise for biosynthesis pathway regulation. According to the results, Inverter_V1.1.1e and Inverter_V1.2.1e with high-activated RFP output are high-level activation circuits. Additionally, Inverter_V1.1.1f and Inverter_V1.2.1f with relatively lower activated RFP output are applicable as low-level activation circuits (Figure 2f and 2g). The diverse performance of inverter variants would allow us to customize pathway regulation strategies to fine-tune the regulation levels in different scenarios for maximized productivity.

Optimization of Genetic Inverter Configurations To implement genetic circuits, one problem is the supply of inducers in the host strain and the other problem is the recruitment of multiple plasmids to deliver the necessary components. Our tryptophan-inducible genetic inverter comprised three plasmids and two transcriptional repressors, which might lead to the above problems in further applications. To address the issues, firstly, the ubiCgene, which catalyzes the conversion of chorismate to 4-hydroxybenzoate and bypasses the tryptophan pathway, was deleted to direct more carbon flux towards tryptophan formation, resulting in the tryptophan-producing strain XG2. Then, the regulatory protein TrpR1(V58K) was integrated into the chromosome of strain XG2 to reduce the number of plasmids, yielding the engineered strain XG3.

To evaluate the functionality of the TrpR(V58K) integrated strain XG3, we investigated the repression and activation functions, respectively. We introduced a simple reporter plasmid (pCS-PtrpO1(G3C)-GFP) into strain XG3 to validate the repression function. As a control, we tested the constitutively expressed PtrpO1(G3C)-Plpp0.03-TrpR1(V58K) system with all genetic elements in two plasmids in strain XG1. Upon induction of tryptophan, the repression within strain XG3 was strong and low-leaky and showed better repression

efficiency (96.67%) than that of the control group (73.07%) (Figure S3). Based on this result, we hypothesized that the low leaky expression would improve the activation function of the inverter. Next, to test the activation function, we transferred two necessity plasmids of Inverter_V1.1.1 into the TrpR(V58K) integrated strain XG3 to form Inverter_V2.1.1. The control group employed Inverter_V1.1.1 harboring three plasmids in strain XG1. As expected, Inverter_V2.1.1e and Inverter_V2.2.1e expressed in high levels with 14.2-fold and 3.4-fold dynamic ranges and reached the highest RFP expression of 5316.5±241.4 a.u. and 5649.1±273.9 a.u., respectively. To summarize, the performance of optimized inverters underscores the necessity of reorganizing inverter configurations. Additionally, based on the results, we proposed that the inverter could be further engineered as a bifunctional regulation circuit for expanded functionalities with repression and activation simultaneously.

###### 5. Characterization of Bifunctional Circuits and Monitoring of Tryptophan Production in Fermentation

To establish the bifunctional circuits, we integrated the original repression function of PtrpO1(G3C)-TrpR1(V58K) into Inverter_V2.1.1 and Inverter_V2.2.1, resulting in strains Dual_V2.1.1 and Dual_V2.2.1. The repression module was constructed by introducing an additional PtrpO1(G3C)promoter-controlled GFP operon (Figure 3d). As the tryptophan concentrations increased, we observed a substantial enhancement in RFP expression and a significant decrease in GFP expression in strains Dual_V2.1.1 and Dual_V2.2.1 (Figure 3e and 3g). They demonstrated low-leaky dynamic activation and high-efficacy dynamic repression curves that aligned with the performance of mono-functional circuits (Figure 3a and S3). Although Dual_V2.1.1 and Dual_V2.2.1 had similar bifunctional performance, Dual_V2.1.1 showed lower leaky expression than Dual_V2.2.1 in the dynamic activation module. However, the culture medium in the test tube showed the orange color at the tryptophan concentration of 31.5 mg/L. We were concerned about the crosstalk between the activation and repression modules, which would lead to interference between RFP and GFP expression. To ensure that each cell performed its functions accurately, we observed the fluorescence of single cells under the microscope. Remarkably, at the low tryptophan concentration, individual cells stably exhibited strong green fluorescence and weak red fluorescence (Figure 3f and 3h). As the tryptophan concentration increased, individual cells gradually showed strong red fluorescence and weak green fluorescence. Overall, the color and intensity of single-cell fluorescence shifted as the tryptophan concentration fluctuated. No interference was observed between the activation module and the repression module.

Through these experiments, we successfully characterized our bifunctional regulation circuits in engineered strain XG3.

Utilizing biosensors to monitor metabolite production can estimate its formation during fermentation without analytical methods (Rogers and Church, 2016; Rogers et al., 2015). To implement the inverter for tryptophan production monitoring, we introduced an additional PLlacO1-controlled trpEfbrGoperon in the medium-copy plasmid to boost tryptophan generation. Next, we introduced an ASV degradation tag (McGinness et al., 2006) to the reporter gene of Inverter_V2.1.1 and Inverter_V2.2.1 to alleviate RFP accumulation, resulting in strains TRPactive_DSA and TRPactive_LAA (Figure 4a). RFP expression was an indicator of tryptophan production. During the early fermentation, tryptophan production increased steadily, resulting in a concurrent rise in RFP expression within both strains (Figure 4b and 4c). At 42 h, when cell growth entered the stationary phase (Figure S4a and S4b), our tryptophan-responsive strains were fully activated and exhibited the highest normalized RFP fluorescence intensity (Figure 4b and 4c). In the later fermentation, tryptophan production slowed, and RFP expression fluctuated slightly but maintained high expression levels. Compared to TRPactive_DSA, TRPactive_LAA exhibited a higher normalized RFP fluorescence intensity when fully activated by tryptophan. The results indicated that these two strains could serve as tryptophan-triggered activation systems.

Next, to test the performance of the bifunctional circuit in fermentation, we introduced additional PLlacO1-controlled trpEfbrGoperon and ASV degradation tag attached RFP into Dual_V2.2.1, yielding tryptophan-responsive strain TRPdual_LAA (Figure 4d). During the log phase of cell growth, the strain exhibited dramatically increased RFP expression as tryptophan formed and slightly increased GFP expression (Figure 4e and S4c). At 30 h, TRPdual_LAA began exhibiting its bifunctionalities with continuously enhanced RFP expression and attenuated GFP expression (Figure 4e). In the stationary phase of cell growth, the strain exhibited RFP activation and GFP repression simultaneously as tryptophan continuously accumulated (Figure 4e and S4c). Interestingly, the expression of the repression target (GFP) was slightly increased first and then repressed as tryptophan accumulation instead of being fully repressed all the time. This repression pattern is useful for gene targets that need initial overexpression but not sustained high expression throughout the entire fermentation process. In other words, the gene within the repression module would overexpress during the cell growth phase to support cellular metabolisms and then be repressed during the cell production phase, potentially conserving cell resources for the desired product formation. The results indicated that strain TRPdual_LAA could be utilized to construct a tryptophan-triggered bifunctional regulation system.

###### 6. Applying Tryptophan-responsive Strain TRPactive_DSA for Dynamic Up-regulation of Tryptamine Production

Metabolite-responsive genetic circuits can be leveraged for dynamic regulation to enhance microbial production. This approach enables autonomous control of metabolic flux, allowing optimized cellular resource allocation (Jiang et al., 2022). Our tryptophan-responsive strain TRPactive_DSA can monitor tryptophan production (Figure 4a), which could be used to develop tryptophan-triggered dynamic activation systems. By substituting the reporter gene

##### AuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscript

(RFP) with a tryptophan derivative synthase or other genes of interest, the system could facilitate two-stage dynamic regulation for efficient production of tryptophan derivatives. In the initial stage, low tryptophan concentration will not trigger heterologous synthase expression to high levels and cell resources will be assigned to endogenous pathway. When tryptophan accumulates sufficiently to fully activate the circuit, heterologous synthase expression will constantly increase to a high level, catalyzing the conversion of tryptophan into the desired end product. The expression of heterologous synthase changes as the tryptophan concentration fluctuates.

To evaluate the tryptophan-triggered dynamic activation system, we used the tryptamine biosynthetic pathway as proof of concept. Given the structural similarities between tryptophan and tryptamine, we first tested the specificity of the TRPactive_DSA strain towards these two compounds. The fluorescence assay results showed that TRPactive_DSA significantly responded only to tryptophan (Figure S5). To demonstrate the efficiency of the dynamic activation system in pathway regulation, we introduced low- and high-level promoters PtetO1-RBS(A2C) and PtetO1-RBS(A8G) into TRPactive_DSA and replaced the reporter gene RFP with the tryptophan decarboxylase from Catharanthus roseus (CrTDC) (Wang et al., 2020), resulting in the tryptamine production strains TM_Low and TM_High, respectively (Figure 5a). In the experimental groups, CrTDC expression was dynamically activated at low or high levels. To compare static and dynamic regulation strategies, we employed TM_Control as the control strain that overexpressed CrTDC by the constitutive promoter Plpp0.5(Figure 5a). As a control, the promoter strength of Plpp0.5 was comparable to the fully activated high-level PtetO1-RBS(A2C) promoter (Figure S6a).

The fermentation results revealed that tryptamine production in dynamic activation groups surpassed the control (Figure 5b). Notably, at 48 h, the low-level dynamic activation strain TM_Low achieved the highest tryptamine titer of 36.9 ± 2.8 mg/L, representing a 2-fold increase compared to the control group TM_Control. This demonstrated that the low-level dynamic activation of CrTDC provided the greatest benefit for tryptamine production within our dynamic activation system. Interestingly, while the control group consumed all tryptophan, the experimental groups had residual tryptophan remaining (Figure S7a). We inferred that the residual tryptophan triggered the dynamic activation system. Then, the system redirected the carbon flux towards tryptamine production, leading to higher tryptamine titers than the control group. These results proved the feasibility of our dynamic activation system for regulating tryptophan derivative production.

###### 7. Applying Tryptophan-responsive Strain TRPdual_LAA for Bifunctional Regulation of Violacein Production

The biosynthesis of valuable plant natural products and secondary metabolites always requires multiple genes or gene clusters to construct the desired biosynthetic pathways (Zhang et al., 2023). However, these complex biosynthesis pathways often contain ratelimiting steps or impose additional metabolic burdens on the host strains, leading to low productivity. Consequently, metabolic regulation (including static and dynamic regulation) is an important factor in establishing efficient microbial cell factories (Hartline et al., 2021; Otero-Muras and Carbonell, 2021). Our bifunctional tryptophan-responsive strain

##### AuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscript

TRPdual_LAA can serve as a dynamic regulation strategy for developing a tryptophantriggered bifunctional regulation system. This system would simultaneously perform dynamic up- and down-regulation for modulating the complex biosynthetic pathways.

As a proof of concept, we introduced the violacein production pathway (Cress et al., 2016), encoded by five heterologous genes, vioA, vioB, vioC, vioD, and vioE, for bifunctional regulation. These five genes were organized and constructed into two separate operons vioACand vioEBD. Firstly, we tested the up-regulation strategy for violacein production. Two violacein synthetic operons, controlled by PtetO1-RBS(A2C) or PtetO1-RBS(A8G) promoters were transfered into strain TRPactive_LAA, generating violacein production strains Vio_HH, Vio_HL, Vio_LL, and Vio_LH (Figure 5c). In Vio_HH, both operons vioACand vioEBDwere under high dynamic expression from PtetO1-RBS(A2C). In Vio_LH, vioEBD was under low dynamic expression from PtetO1-RBS(A8G), while vioAC was under high dynamic expression from PtetO1-RBS(A2C). Other strains followed a similar naming convention. The control group constitutively overexpressed the two operons using the Plpp0.5constitutive promoter, resulting in strain Vio_0.5. The strength of the Plpp0.5promoter was comparable to the fully activated high-level PtetO1-RBS(A2C) promoter (Figure S6b). At 48 h, violacein production in the dynamic up-regulation groups Vio_LH and Vio_HH exceeded the control group by 4.5-fold and 5.7-fold, respectively (Figure 5d). The results indicated that high-level dynamic up-regulation of the vioACoperon was more beneficial to violacein production. Although the control group Vio_0.5 exhibited less residual tryptophan than the dynamic control groups (Figure S7b), it still cannot achieve a higher violacein titer. This suggested that the dynamic regulation approach enabled more efficient cellular resource utilization and carbon flux distribution.

Next, we further incorporated the dynamic down-regulation strategy for bifunctional regulation of violacein production. Given the repression pattern of our tryptophan-triggered bifunctional regulation system (Figure 4e), the expression level of the repression target would initially increase and then gradually decrease during fermentation. Therefore, the choice of the repression target was critical. Since violacein is a hydrophobic bis-indole pigment, it accumulates in the cell membrane compartment after formation. For optimized violacein production, membrane engineering strategies have been employed to increase membrane area by boosting the membrane synthetic pathway, or to add extra intracellular reservoirs by generating lip droplets through improved triacylglycerol accumulation (Ma et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2021). A previous study demonstrated that overexpression of plsB, a crucial gene in the membrane synthetic pathway, improved deoxyviolacein production and altered the morphology of the E. colicell membrane (Yang et al., 2021). Considering the similar chemical property of deoxyviolacein and violacein, plsB was selected as our dynamic down-regulation target. We expected that the regulation system would initially direct carbon flux for cell membrane buildup and cell growth, followed by a shift towards violacein generation.

We then constructed an additional repression operon harboring the PtrpO1(G3C)promotercontrolled plsBgene in strain Vio_HH, resulting in violacein production strain Viodual_HH with bifunctional regulation (Figure 5e). As a control, we used the Vio_HH-0.5 strain with single up-regulation and constitutively expressed plsBgene by using the Plpp0.5

##### AuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscript

promoter. The strength of the Plpp0.5promoter was slightly higher than the PtrpO1(G3C) promoter (Figure S6c), making it suitable for the control group configuration. After 48 h of fermentation, the strain Viodual_HH with up-regulated violacein synthetic operons and down-regulated plsBgene further promoted violacein production, yielding 10.5±0.6 mg/L violacein. This titer was 7.4 folds higher than that of the unregulated control strain (Vio_0.5, 1.4±0.1 mg/L) and 1.3 folds higher than the single up-regulation control strain (Vio_HH-0.5, 8.0±0.4 mg/L) (Figure 5f). Additionally, the strain Viodual_HH consumed more tryptophan than the strain Vio_HH-0.5 (Figure S7c), indicating a more efficient utilization of the carbon flux. Overall, these fermentation results demonstrated the effectiveness of our bifunctional regulation system in controlling complex tryptophan-derived pathways.

#### Discussion

Transcriptional factor-based biosensors have been widely established, characterized, and implemented as useful tools for dynamic regulation (Choi et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the applications of transcriptional repressor-based biosensors are often limited by their inherent repression functions. To address this issue, this study introduced a design logic to construct transcriptional repressor-based inverters for expanded functionalities and broader applications. This approach integrated two transcriptional repressors to achieve activation outputs. As proof of concept, a tryptophan-inducible genetic inverter was constructed by assembling PtrpO1(G3C)-TrpR1(V58K) and PtetO1-TetR repression systems. However, after assembling the necessary genetic elements in plasmids, the initial Inverter_V1 did not show obvious activation output. To achieve robust activation output, we engineered the genetic elements of the inverter by introducing degradation tags, RBS engineering, and promoter engineering. Remarkably, the Inverter_V1.2.1e variant achieved the highest RFP expression level among all variants with moderate leaky expression. Subsequently, we made two crucial modifications to the host strain to better leverage the inverters, including the deletion of tryptophan bypass and the genomic integration of a portion of the inverter. First, we deleted the ubiC gene to increase tryptophan production. Second, we integrated the TrpR1(V58K) regulatory protein directly into the chromosome, reducing the number of plasmids required for the inverter. These manipulations and optimizations enhanced the dynamic performance of the inverter, generating improved variants suitable for diverse applications.

We first developed dynamic activation systems to evaluate the inverter applications by directly adopting the optimized inverter variants. The systems enabled tryptophan production monitoring and dynamic up-regulation of tryptamine production. Interestingly, the fermentation results showed that the highest tryptamine titer was achieved by strain with low-level dynamic regulation of tryptamine synthase, rather than high-level dynamic regulation. This suggested that the high-level dynamic regulation strategy did not improve productivity when enzyme activity was robust. Instead, the optimal outcome was achieved by implementing the most suitable dynamic regulation pattern, which directed carbon flux and cellular resources in a way that yielded the highest product titer. These results highlighted the importance of expanding the inverter repository and customizing dynamic regulation systems to adapt to different scenarios.

##### AuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscriptAuthorManuscript

Moreover, we developed a bifunctional regulation system by employing a bifunctional circuit that combined the original repression function of PtrpO1(G3C)-TrpR1(V58K) with the activation function of the inverter. To demonstrate its application for complex pathway regulation, we selected the violacein pathway as a case study. A previous study identified plsB as an overexpression target to enhance deoxyviolacein production by changing cell morphology (Yang et al., 2021). Herein, we dynamically expressed the plsB gene. Based on the activation and repression patterns observed in the bifunctional circuit, we downregulated the plsB gene and up-regulated the violacein synthetic genes. This regulation strategy first enabled the expansion of cell storage capability, and then redirected carbon flux towards violacein production, thereby optimizing the utilization of cellular resources. Finally, this bifunctional regulation system achieved a 7.4-fold increase in violacein titer.

In this study, we successfully constructed a tryptophan-inducible transcriptional repressorbased genetic inverter with exceptional properties and wide-ranging applications. Genetic circuit engineering plays a vital role in improving the inverter performance, encompassing efforts to enhance dynamic range and optimize the expression level. The utilization of optimized inverter variants enabled the dynamic regulation of tryptophan derivatives, resulting in enhanced production titers. In conclusion, our research demonstrated the feasibility and promise of constructing transcription repressor-based inverters. Herein, we only utilized two specific transcriptional repressors as the proof-of-concept. Moving forward, more qualified transcriptional repressors could be applied to our design. This would generate a more diverse repository of transcriptional repressor-based genetic inverters, further broadening the applications of these repressors in metabolic engineering.

###### 3. Fluorescence assays

To evaluate the fluorescence outputs of the initial inverter variants, the corresponding plasmids were transferred into strain XG1. For the test of optimized inverter variants with simplified configurations, the corresponding plasmids were transferred into strain XG3. Three independent colonies of each testing group were randomly selected and inoculated into test tubes with 3 mL of fresh LB medium with appropriate antibiotics to prepare seed cultures. After overnight cultivation (10–12 h) at 37 °C at 270 rpm, the seed cultures were transferred into test tubes with 3 mL of fresh M9–2Y medium with gradient tryptophan (0, 31.5, 125, 500 mg/L) and appropriate antibiotics with an inoculation ratio of 1% (30 μL). The new cultures were then placed at 37 °C at 270 rpm. After 24 h of cultivation, samples were taken from the cultures, diluted 10 folds, and subjected to cell density (OD600) measurements and red or green fluorescence intensities using a 96-well plate reader (Synergy HT, BioTek). Red and green fluorescence intensities were measured in arbitrary units (a.u.). All experiments were performed in triplicates, and the data were presented as the averages and standard deviations (n=3).

###### 4. Cell imaging

The E. coliimaging for Dual_V2.1.1 and Dual_V2.2.1 was performed using a custombuilt light-sheet microscope (Song et al., 2024). This microscope incorporated a 16x/0.8 NA water-immersion detection objective from Nikon (N16XLWD-PF). eGFP fluorescence excitation was achieved with a 488 nm laser, operating at a power density of 1.5 W/cm2. Similarly, RFP fluorescence was excited using a 561 nm laser, also set to a power density of 1.5 W/cm2. The light sheet’s full-width at half maximum (FWHM) at the beam waist measured 6.7 μm. An exposure time of 50 ms was maintained during imaging. The resultant volumetric images were of dimensions 276.48 × 276.48 × 5 μm3, corresponding to a resolution of 2048 × 2048 × 17 pixels or 0.313 × 0.313 × 1.64 μm3 per pixel. For the preparation of the samples, E. colicells (XG3 with Dual_V2.1.1 and Dual_V2.2.1) were immobilized in a 1% low melting point agarose solution (Sigma) mixed with 1% PBS (Gibco). The samples were then placed within a petri dish to ensure stability during the imaging process.

###### 5. Shake flasks fermentation

To explore the performance of inverter variants in dynamic up-regulation of tryptamine, the relevant plasmids were transformed into strain XG3. IPTG was added to the cultures at the final concentration of 0.5 mM to induce the expression of PLlacO1-controlled trpEfbrG operon. The cultures were then grown at 30 °C at 270 rpm for 48 h.

To explore the performance of inverter variants and bifunctional circuits in the dynamic regulation of violacein, the relevant plasmids were transformed into strain XG3. Three colonies of the transformed strains were picked from LB plates and inoculated into 3 mL of LB medium overnight at 37 °C at 270 rpm for 10–12 h. 6. HPLC analysis Collected fermentation samples (500 μL) were diluted with 500 μL methanol and incubated at 55 °C for 10 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were filtered through 0.22 μm syringe filters before HPLC analysis. The concentrations of tryptophan, tryptamine, and violacein were quantitatively analyzed by using the Agilent HPLC 1260 Infinity II system equipped with a 1260 Infinity II Diode Array Detector WR and a reverse-phase ZORBAX SB-C18 column.

For tryptophan and tryptamine quantification, the injection volume was 5 μL per sample. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (v/v) as solvent A and 100% methanol as solvent B. The flow rate of the mobile phase was 1 mL/min. The gradient elution was as follows: 5%−40% solvent B for 15 min, 40%−5% solvent B for 1 min, and 5% solvent B for an additional 4 min. The column temperature was set at 25 °C. Tryptophan and tryptamine were detected at 276 nm with retention times of 13.8 min and 12.9 min, respectively. Standard curves were prepared using gradient concentrations of tryptophan and tryptamine solutions in methanol.

For violacein quantification, the injection volume was also 5 μL per sample. The mobile phase and flow rate were the same as above. The gradient elution was as follows: 5%−80% solvent B for 10 min, 80% solvent B for 4 min, 80%−5% solvent B for 4 min, and 5% solvent B for an additional 2 min. The column temperature was set at 25 °C. Violacein was detected at 570 nm with a retention time of 12.2 min. A standard curve was generated using gradient concentrations of violacein solution in methanol.
